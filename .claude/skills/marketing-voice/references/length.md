# Length and density

## Ceilings by content type

| Content type | Body words | What buys the extra room |
|---|---|---|
| How-to guide | 800–1,200 | One reader, one task, one path |
| Comparison against one named rival | 1,000–1,400 | Two products, one decision |
| Roundup of a category | 1,200–1,600 | More products, each covered once |
| Roundup with a full table, a dozen products | 1,800–2,200 | The table itself, and a profile per product |
| Engineering explainer (Register C) | 2,000–3,000 | Difficulty the reader came for |

Body words exclude frontmatter and code fences and include the table. Register C is the only type where length is earned by the subject being genuinely hard; everywhere else the ceiling is a ceiling.

A roundup gets more room than a how-to because it covers more products, not because it says more about each one. Going over the ceiling is justified by another product or another citation, never by more prose about the ones already there.

## Short sentences do not make a short article

Every article this guide governed passed rule 8 and still read as bloated. Rule 8 measures sentences and paragraphs. Nothing was measuring sections, and that is where the words were.

A 3,000-word page built from 13-word sentences is still a 3,000-word page. The failure mode is redundant sections, not long sentences — so the fix is deletion, not rewording.

Compressing must not trade one failure for another. Welding three short sentences into one long one lowers the word count and raises the median, which fails rule 8. Cut whole sections instead.

## The patterns that produce the bloat

**The same claim in the intro, the body and the FAQ.** A roundup says it a fourth time in the segmented verdict. Pick the one place the reader is actually deciding, and delete the other three.

**Feature enumeration where only the deciding difference matters.** If a table already carries the field, the prose beside it must add judgement or go. A profile that restates its own table row is the table twice.

**Preamble before the verdict.** Methodology, scope-setting and "how we compared these" belong beside the sources at the end, or nowhere. The reader came for the answer, not for the reassurance that we were careful.

**Sections that exist for completeness.** Written because the outline had a slot, not because a reader needed it. A closing recommendation that restates every profile above it is the clearest case.

## The cutting test

Delete the section. Can the reader still do the thing, or still make the decision?

If yes, it stays deleted. If the only loss is that the page feels less thorough, it stays deleted — thoroughness the reader does not use is length they pay for.

For a paragraph beside a table, the narrower question: what does this let the reader decide that the table did not?

## What never gets cut to make the number

- A product from a roundup. A rival's "7 Tools Compared" that ships a table missing two of them is the defect we are competing against
- An honest concession that a competitor beats us at something
- A third-party claim's source link, or the dated "checked on" statement beside the sources
- The "not published" answer where a vendor publishes no figure
- Our own "where it lets you down" slot

If the count only comes down by dropping one of these, the piece is the wrong shape rather than the wrong length. Say so instead of cutting.
